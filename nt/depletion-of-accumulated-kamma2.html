Notes on Dhamma

The Depletion of Old Kamma

  • A Saddhamma Study Group translation from the Pāḷi text.
    Get PDFs

In the kammanirodha sutta (SN 35.146), the Buddha gives the four noble truths in terms of kamma as follows:

O bhikkhus, I will teach you new and old kamma, the cessation of kamma, and the path leading to the cessation of kamma. Listen to that and do mind it well, I will speak.”

And what, O bhikkhus, is old kamma? The eye, O bhikkhus, is old kamma, especially conditioned, especially intended, to be seen as something to be felt. The ear…. The nose…. The tongue…. The body…. The mind, O bhikkhus, is old kamma, especially conditioned, especially intended, to be seen as something to be felt. This, O bhikkhus, is called old kamma.

And what, O bhikkhus, is new kamma? O bhikkhus, whatever action is done by body, speech, or mind in the present [life]. This, O bhikkhus, is called new kamma.

And what, O bhikkhus, is the cessation of kamma? [That] which touches liberation through the cessation of bodily action, verbal action and mental action. This, O bhikkhus, is called the cessation of kamma.

“And what, O bhikkhus, is the path leading to the cessation of kamma? It is just this noble eightfold path, that is – right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right samadhi. This, O bhikkhus, is called the path leading to the cessation of kamma.
–SN 35.146

So then, old kamma pertains to kamma done and accumulated in past lives to be felt via the six senses. New kamma pertains to kamma done and accumulated in this present life. The cessation of kamma pertains to both old and new kamma and is defined as that which touches liberation through the cessation of bodily, verbal and mental action. The path leading to the cessation of kamma is just this noble eight-fold path.

The definitions of old kamma, new kamma and the path leading to the cessation of kamma are all quite clear in this sutta. The definition of the cessation of kamma (kammanirodha), however, needs more clarification.

These Dhamma notes are intended to clarify the meaning of kammanirodha (Section 1) and its implications for the designation of the arahant’s actions (Section 2).

The Buddha makes a distinction between two types of kamma in the paṭhamanidāna sutta (AN 3.111), namely, kamma that leads to the origination of kamma, which is any kamma born of greed, hatred or delusion; and kamma that leads to the cessation of kamma, which is any kamma born of non-greed non-hatred or non-delusion.

He states in the nidāna sutta (AN 3.34): “Whatever kamma is born of greed, hatred or delusion, wherever self-begetting is actualized, it is there that the kamma ripens. Wherever that kamma ripens, it is there that the results are experienced, either in this life or in the [next] arising or in some other [arisings] in succession.”

“Whatever kamma is born of non-greed, non-hatred or non-delusion, that kamma is abandoned when greed, hatred and delusion are eliminated. It is cut off at the root, made like palm stump, obliterated so that it is no longer subject to future arising.”

Since the Buddha’s definition of the cessation of kamma refers to both the cessation of old kamma and the cessation of new kamma, we first look at how the cessation of old kamma comes about.

Cessation of Old Kamma

In the sancetana sutta (AN 10.217), the Buddha makes it clear that without experiencing all kamma accumulated in past lives, the attainment of arahantship is not possible:

“I, O bhikkhus, do not say that [there is] the termination of intentional kamma accumulated in past [lives] without having experienced [it], and that is in this [present] life, or in the [next] arising or in some other [arisings] in succession. But still I, O bhikkhus, do not say [there is] the making of an end of suffering without having experienced intentional kamma accumulated in past [lives].”
–AN 10.217

Since all kamma that lead to the cessation of kamma get destroyed after they have served their purpose of eliminating greed, hatred and delusion as stated in the nidāna sutta (AN 3.34), the Buddha’s declaration can be paraphrased as follows: Without experiencing all kamma born of greed, hatred or delusion accumulated in past lives, the attainment of arahantship is not possible.

This is stated in another way in the pāṭibhoga sutta (AN 4.182), where the Buddha declares that just as for what is liable to death, there is neither a samana nor brahmana nor Deva nor Mara nor Brahma nor anyone in the world who can guarantee that it will not die, so too for those evil kamma which are defiling, renewing a being, dreadful, ripening in suffering, bound up with future birth, old age and death, there is neither a samana nor brahmana nor Deva nor Mara nor Brahma nor anyone in the world who can guarantee that their results will not actualize.

Likewise, the following verses from the dhammapada make it clear that kamma and death cannot be escaped as follows:

Neither in the air, nor the middle of the ocean,
nor going into an open cleft in a mountain; 127
a place in the world does not exists,
where staying one could be released from evil kamma.

Neither in the air, nor the middle of the ocean,
nor going into an open cleft in a mountain, 128
a place in the world does not exists,
where staying one is not overpowered by death.
–Dhp 127-128

Here, the Buddha is saying that just as one who is born cannot escape death, so too one who has done evil kamma cannot escape its results. This “inescapability of unwholesome accumulated kamma” declaration that without experiencing all kamma born of greed, hatred or delusion accumulated in past lives, the attainment of arahantship is not possible, presents a problem for the commentators of the dhammapada verses and the jataka verses. The commentator of the dhammapada verses claim that the venerable Mahāmoggallāna’s death was caused by his past life evil kamma of killing his parents. On the other hand, the commentator of the jataka verses claim that the venerable Mahāmoggallāna’s death was caused by his past life evil kamma of intending to kill his parents but changing his mind later and not carrying through with his intentions (see the venerable Anandajoti’s translationof the dhammapada verses commentaries). The commentators’ contradiction of the Buddha’s declaration implies that the venerable Mahāmoggallāna could not have been an arahant if the Buddha’s declaration is interpreted as stated. Since it cannot be denied that the venerable Mahāmoggallāna was an arahant, the Anguttāra Nikāya commentator asserts that the Buddha’s declaration should not be interpreted as stated. The venerable Bhikkhu Bodhi translates the commentator’s interpretation in his notes on the Buddha’s declaration as follows:

Mp explains the text’s statement as having an implicit meaning: “This is intended to show that as long as saṃsāra continues, if there is kamma that has acquired the capacity to ripen (paṭiladdhavipākārahakamma) ‘there is no place on earth where one might escape an evil deed’” (the citation, na vijjati so jagatippadeso, yatthaṭṭhito mucceyya pāpakammā, is from [Dhp 127]
–Venerable Bhikkhu Bodhi’s notes on AN 10.217

The full verses [Dhp 127-128] that the Anguttāra Nikāya commentator eluded to in his explanation is quoted above. In those verses, even though the Buddha is comparing the inescapability of accumulated evil deeds to the inescapability of death, the commentator wants us to believe that when the Buddha says “accumulated past [life] kamma (kammānaṃ katānaṃ upacitānaṃ, lit. accumulated kamma done)”, he really means to say “only kamma that has acquired the capacity to ripen,” meaning that if the kamma has not acquired the capacity to ripen, then the arahant can escape it and render it ownerless kamma (ahosikamma), never to ripen, since the arahant cannot be born again.

If we accept the commentator’s interpretation and amend the Buddha’s declaration accordingly, then the first part of the declaration would read:

nāhaṃ bhikkhave sañcetanikānaṃ paṭiladdhavipākārahakamma appaṭisaṃviditvā vyantībhāvaṃ vadāmi. tañca kho diṭṭhe va dhamme upapajje vā apare vā pariyāye.

It translates as:

I, O bhikkhus, do not say that [there is] the termination of intentional kamma that has acquired the capacity to ripen [in this life] without having experienced [it], and that is in this life, or in the [next] arising or in some other [arisings] in succession. 

But if the Buddha really means to say only kamma that has acquired the capacity to ripen in this life, then there will be no need for him to state the possibility of it ripening in the next [arising] or some other [arisings] in succession since the arahant cannot be born any where after death. As the Buddha states in the niruttipatha sutta (SN 22.62), wise ascetics and brahmins do not mix up the past, the future and the present in their declarations as proposed by the Anguttāra Nikāya commentator.

Another argument brought forth by the proponents of ahosikamma, is that it is impossible to experience all the evil kamma accumulated in past lives because this saṃsāra has no known beginning, and so all beings must have accumulated an infinite number of evil kamma in their past lives, which they claim should take an infinite number of lives to experience its results, making the attainment of arahantship impossible if the Buddha’s inescapable kamma declaration is to be interpreted as stated. But although the Buddha states that the understanding of the ripening of kamma is only within the powers of the Buddhas, he provides some guidelines for ruling out any interpretation of the ripening of kamma which would make living the life of purity for rightly making an end to suffering impossible, as wrong interpretation in the loṇaphala sutta (AN 3.100) as follows:

Whoever, O bhikkhus, might say thus: “In which ever way that a person creates a kamma, in just that way he experiences.” That being so, O bhikkhus, there is not the living the life of purity and an opportunity for rightly making an end of suffering is not evident.

But whoever, O bhikkhus, might say thus: “In which ever way to be felt that a person creates a kamma, in just that way he feels the results.” That being so, O bhikkhus, there is the living the life of purity and an opportunity for rightly making an end of suffering is evident.

Thus, such linear interpretations of the fruition of kamma which negates the living of the life of purity for rightly making an end to suffering, can be ruled out as wrong interpretations.

In the mahāpuṇṇama sutta (MN 109) the Buddha proclaims the saṅkhāra appropriable khandha to be any type of saṅkhāra whatsoever, whether past, future or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, far or near, all sankharas and anything pertaining to sankharas such as kamma, are included in the saṅkhāra appropriable khandha, whether finite or infinite is irrelevant. As the Buddha declares in the nidāna sutta verses (AN 3.34), the amount of kamma done in past lives born of greed, hatred and delusion, whether little or much is inconsequential. All of it is to be felt right here in this life as no other ground exists for their fruition for one who has given rise to noble knowledge. In other words, whether the amount of kamma done in the past in this long saṃsāra without discoverable beginning is infinite or finite, it is still bounded by the saṅkhāra khandha and so cannot escape depletion when the saṅkhāra khandha goes to depletion. Even saṃsāra itself does not lie outside of the five appropriable khandhas, even though it has no known beginning.

The notion of ahosikamma is a popular fallacy among commentators and scholars alike. It asserts that the owner of a kamma can escape its results and render that kamma ownerless, never to ripen. This is simply a wishful thinking which is nowhere to be found in the Suttas or the Vinaya. On the contrary, as stated in the pāṭibhoga sutta (AN 4.182), the Buddha declares that just as for what is liable to death, there is neither a samana nor brahmana nor Deva nor Mara nor Brahma nor anyone in the world who can guarantee that it will not die, so too for those evil kamma which are defiling, renewing a being, dreadful, ripening in suffering, bound up with future birth, old age and death, neither a samana nor brahmana nor Deva nor Mara nor Brahma nor anyone in the world can guarantee that their results will not actualize, let alone proponents of ahosikamma.

As the Buddha declares in the cūḷakammavibhanga sutta:

Beings, O Young Brahmin, are owners of their kamma, heirs of their kamma, born of their kamma, descendants of their kamma, have kamma as their refuge.

Thus, one remains the only owner of one’s kamma born of greed, hatred or delusion until one eliminates it by experiencing its results without any residue remaining; or else if it is kamma born of non-greed, non-hatred or non-delusion, then one eliminates greed, hatred and delusion in order to cut that kamma off at the root, make it like a palm stump, obliterate it so that it is no longer subject to future arising. The Buddha does not declare any other means of getting rid of the debt of accumulated kamma.

Given that evil kamma accumulated in past lives cannot be escaped by any one in the world against the wishes of proponents of ahosikamma, how should the meaning of the Buddha’s definition of the cessation of kamma be understood in light of the declaration about the inescapability of accumulated unwholesome kamma? As noted, the Buddha’s definition of the cessation of kamma obviously refers to the cessation of both old kamma and new kamma. Thus, we need to answer the following questions:

  1. How does the experiencing of the results of the intentional kamma accumulated in past lives come about in order to make an end of suffering and become an arahant?

  2. What about the results of the intentional kamma accumulated in the present life? Does the arahant have to experience the results of all the intentional kamma accumulated in the present life as well or can the arahant escape their results?

Now to answer question 1. “How does the experiencing of the results of accumulated past lives kamma come about in order to make an end of suffering and become an arahant?”

Since it is not possible to make an end of suffering and become an arahant without first making the breakthrough to the four noble truths to become a sotāpanna (see kūṭāgāra sutta SN 56.44) we address the question as to whether the depletion of accumulated past kamma happens only prior to the attainment of arahantship or are some of the accumulated past kamma required to undergo depletion prior to the attainment of sotāpanna as well.

In the nibbedhika sutta (AN 6.63), the Buddha defines the diversity of āsava and the diversity of kamma to be equivalent as follows:

And what, O bhikkhus, is the diversity of asavas? There are, O bhikkhus, asavas leading to hell; there are asavas leading to the animal womb; there are asavas leading to the realm of ghosts; there are asavas leading to the world of human beings; there are asavas leading to the deva world. This, O bhikkhus, is called the diversity of asavas.

And what, O bhikkhus, is the diversity of kamma? There is, O bhikkhus, kamma to be experienced in hell; there is kamma to be experienced in the animal womb; there is kamma to be experienced in the realm of ghosts; there is kamma to be experienced in the world of human beings; there is kamma to be experienced in the deva world. This, O bhikkhus, is called the diversity of kamma.

In the sabbāsava sutta, the Buddha says that there are āsavas to be abandoned by seeing the four noble truths:

It is from knowing, O bhikkhus, from seeing, that the extinction of the asavas come to be, I say, not from unknowing, not from unseeing. But knowing what, O bhikkhus, seeing what, does the extinction of the asavas come to be? Attention to the root-source and attention to the non-root-source. From attending to the non-root-source, O bhikkhus, unarisen asavas arise and arisen asavas increase. But from attending to the root-source, O bhikkhus, unarisen asavas do not arise and arisen asavas get abandoned...

He attends to the root-source thus: “This is suffering”; he attends to the root-source thus: “This is the origination of suffering”; he attends to the root-source thus: “This is the cessation of suffering”; he attends to the root-source thus: “This is the path leading to the cessation of suffering.”Of him attending to the root-source thus, three fetters get abandoned: the self-affirmation view, doubt, strong hold on virtue and practices. These, O bhikkhus, are called the asavas to be abandoned by seeing.

From this sutta, we know that along with the three fetters that get abandoned by the sotāpanna, the āsavas leading to hell, the āsavas leading to the animal womb, and the āsavas leading to the realm of ghosts, also goes to depletion. Consequently, the termination through feeling the results of intentional kamma accumulated in past lives to be experienced in hell, in the animal womb or in the realm of ghosts must precede the attainment of sotāpanna.

This is stated in terms of lust, hatred and delusion in the appahāya sutta (AN 6.89) as follows:

Without having abandoned six dhammas, O bhikkhus, one is incapable of realizing accomplishment in view. Which six?

Self-affirmation view, doubt, strong hold on virtue and practices, lust leading to the lower worlds, hatred leading to the lower worlds, delusion leading to the lower worlds.

Without having abandoned these six dhammas, O bhikkhus, one is incapable of realizing accomplishment in view.

Having abandoned six dhammas, O bhikkhus, one is capable of realizing accomplishment in view. Which six?

Self-affirmation view, doubt, strong hold on virtue and practices, lust leading to the lower worlds, hatred leading to the lower worlds, delusion leading to the lower worlds.

Having abandoned these six dhammas, O bhikkhus, one is capable of realizing accomplishment in view.
–AN 6.89

The one accomplished in view is the sotāpanna. This is just restating what has been pointed out regarding the sabbasava sutta (MN 2), that it is impossible to realize the fruit of stream entry without eliminating the āsavas born of lust leading to the lower worlds, the āsavas born of hatred leading to the lower worlds, the āsavas born of delusion leading to the lower worlds.

This is nicely summarized in the venerable samitigutta theragāthā verse (Tha 81) as follows:

yaṃ mayā pakataṃ pāpaṃ,
pubbe aññāsu jātisu;
idheva taṃ vedanīyaṃ,
vatthu aññaṃ na vijjatīti.

Whatever evil [kamma] I have done,
in previous other births;
that is to be experienced right here,
no other place exists for it.
–Tha 81

In the kamma sutta (Ud 3.1) we get the following account illustrating this as well:

Thus was heard by me. At one time the Auspicious One was dwelling at Savatthi in Jeta’s Grove, Anathapindika’s Park. On that occasion a certain bhikkhu was sitting not far from the Auspicious One, having crossed his legs, having directed his body straight, having established mindfulness on the entire field of perception [internally], he was forbearing feelings borne of past kamma results that are painful, piercing, sharp, being mindful and contemplative, without distress.

The Auspicious One saw that bhikkhu sitting not far, having crossed his legs, having directed his body straight, having established mindfulness on the entire field of perception [internally], forbearing feelings borne of past kamma results that are painful, piercing, sharp, being mindful and contemplative, without distress.

Then the Auspicious One, having understood the significance of this, uttered this inspired utterance:

For the bhikkhu who is shaking off the dust of past kamma,
the abandoning of all kamma;
For one like that, disowning, unmoved,
There is no ambition to chitchat with people.
–Ud 3.1

Another example of the sotāpanna’s depletion of kamma leading to the lower worlds is summarized in the following verses that ends the nidāna sutta (AN 3.34):

lobhajaṃ dosajaṃ ceva,
mohajaṃ cāpaviddasu;
yaṃ tena pakataṃ kammaṃ,
appaṃ vā yadi vā bahuṃ;
idheva taṃ vedanīyaṃ,
vatthu aññaṃ na vijjati.

tasmā lobhaṃ ca dosaṃ ca,
mohajaṃ cāpi viddasu;
vijjaṃ uppādayaṃ bhikkhu,
sabbā duggatiyo jahe’ti.

“Whatever kamma that was done,
born of greed, hatred and delusion,
by a person not knowing [noble knowledge];
whether little or much,
that is to be felt right here;
other ground for it does not exist.

From that the wise bhikkhu,
giving rise to noble knowledge;
abandons all bad destinations,
born of greed, hatred and also delusion.
–AN 3.34

Once all kamma accumulated in past lives to be experienced in hell, the animal womb or the realm of ghosts are terminated through feeling their results, it becomes impossible for the sotāpanna to create new bodily, verbal and mental kamma to be experienced in the lower worlds as stated in the abhabba sutta (AN 6.91):

O bhikkhus, the individual accomplished in view is unable to give rise to these six dhammas. Which six?

Self-affirmation view, doubt, strong hold on virtue and practices, lust leading to the lower worlds, hate leading to the lower worlds, delusion leading to the lower worlds.

These, O bhikkhus, are the six dhammas which an individual accomplished in view is unable to give rise to.

After the depletion of all past lives accumulated kamma, all present life accumulated kamma to be experienced in hell, the animal womb or the realm of ghosts are also to be experienced in this very life before death without residue remaining. Since for the owner of that kamma, there is no longer any where else existing for their experience, they cannot accompany the sotāpanna after death.

In the saḷāyatanavibhaṅga sutta (MN 137), the sotāpanna who has experienced all the kamma to be experienced in hell, the animal womb or the realm of ghosts in this very life is contrasted with the Dhamma-deaf puthujjana as one who has not conquered the results (vipāka) [of evil kamma] being blind to their danger (avipākajinassa anādīnavadassāvino assutavato puthujjanassa). The Buddha gives a great simile for the suffering that such a sotāpanna eliminates, in the nakhasikhā sutta (SN 13.1) as follows:

Thus was heard by me. At one time the Auspicious One was dwelling at Savatthi in Jeta’s Grove, Anathapindika’s Park. Then the Auspicious One, having put a small soil on the tip of his fingernail, addressed the bhikkhus: “So what do you think, O bhikkhus, which is more? This small soil put on the tip of a fingernail by me, or this great earth?”

“This, O Bhante, is clearly more, namely the great earth. This is a trifle, insignificant, this small soil the Auspicious One has put on the tip of his fingernail. In comparison with the great earth, this small soil the Auspicious One has put on the tip of his fingernail does not even count, it does not even come close to comparison, it does not even approach part of a fraction.”

It is just so, O bhikkhus, for the noble disciple, for the one accomplished in view. For the individual who has made the breakthrough, just this is more, namely, the suffering that is completely extincted, exhausted, [while the suffering] left over is insignificant. In comparison with the former stock of suffering that is completely extincted, exhausted, this does not even count, it does not even come close to comparison, it does not even approach part of a fraction, namely, the seven lives at most [remaining].

“Thus, O bhikkhus, is the great benefit of making the breakthrough to the Dhamma, thus is the great benefit of acquiring the Dhamma eye.”

So similar to the Buddha’s declaration that without experiencing all unwholesome kamma accumulated in past lives, the attainment of arahantship is not possible, the termination through feeling the results of intentional kamma accumulated in past lives to be experienced in hell, in the animal womb or in the realm of ghosts precedes the attainment of sotāpanna.

In light of the Buddha’s definition of the cessation of kamma being “that which touches liberation from the cessation of bodily, verbal and mental kamma,” and kamma is defined as intention which has contact as its origin (see the nibbedhika sutta, AN 6.63), the cessation of contact is that which touches liberation from the cessation of bodily, verbal and mental action.

In the kalahavivāda sutta (Snp 4.11), the Buddha is asked about how one abides in the cessation of contact where “touches don’t touch”. He answers that it is for one who abides in such a way that form does not come to be, that “touches don’t touch” (rūpe vibhūte na phusanti phassā).

What is the source of contact in the world,
and also from where [comes] possessiveness?
When what is not does mine-ness not exists?
From the unbeing of what do touches not touch?

On account of name-and-form, there is contact.
Possessiveness springs from desire,
desire not being, mine-ness does not come to be.
From the unbeing of form, touches don’t touch.

How has one attained to the unbeing of form,
or even pleasure and pain, how do they unbe?
Do declare this to me how they unbe,
let us know that, thus was my thought.

Not percipient of perception, not percipient of abnormal perception
also not non-percipient, not devoid of being percipient.
For one attained thus, there is the unbeing of form;
because subjective elaborations have perception as their source. –Snp 4.11

The mahāsaḷāyatanika sutta, (MN 149) describes how all accumulated five appropriable khandhas go to depletion for the attainment of arahantship by one who abides in such a way that there is the unbeing of form:

But O bhikkhus, one who knows and sees the eye as it has come to be, one who knows and sees forms as they have come to be, one who knows and sees eye-consciousness as it has come to be, one who knows and sees eye-contact as it has come to be, and this feeling which arises in dependence on eye-contact, whether pleasant or painful or neither-painful-nor-pleasant, that too one who knows and sees it as it has come to be, is not tainted in the eye, is not tainted in forms, is not tainted in eye-consciousness, is not tainted in eye-contact, and this feeling which arises in dependence on eye-contact, whether pleasant or painful or neither-painful-nor-pleasant, that too he is not tainted in it.

And for he who abides untainted, unattached, mindful, seeing in accordance with disadvantage, his five appropriable khandhas go to depletion for future [being]; and his thirsting—the renewer of being, accompanied by lusting after delight, delighting now here now there—is abandoned. His bodily stresses are abandoned, his mental stresses are abandoned, his bodily torments are abandoned, his mental torments are abandoned, his bodily fevers are abandoned, his mental fevers are abandoned, and he experiences bodily and mental pleasure.

He who has come to be of such view, that is his right view. He who has come to be of such thought, that is his right thought. He who has come to be of such effort, that is his right effort. He who has come to be of such mindfulness, that is his right mindfulness. He who has come to be of such samadhi, that is his right samadhi. But his bodily actions, verbal actions and livelihood are well purified just earlier. Thus, of him, this noble eight-fold path goes to full maturation by enculturation. From his maturation of the noble eight-fold path, the four establishments of mindfulness also go to full maturation by enculturation. The four right strivings also go to full maturation by enculturation. The four bases for psychic powers also go to full maturation by enculturation. The five faculties also go to full maturation by enculturation. The five powers also go to full maturation by enculturation. The seven awakening factors also go to full maturation by enculturation.

Of him these two dhammas turn yoked together: samatha and vipassana. He comprehensively knows by supreme knowledge those dhammas that should be comprehensively known by supreme knowledge. He abandons by supreme knowledge those dhammas that should be abandoned by supreme knowledge. He brings into being by supreme knowledge those dhammas that should be brought into being by supreme knowledge. He realizes by supreme knowledge those dhammas that should be realized by supreme knowledge.

And what, O bhikkhus, are the dhammas that should be comprehensively known by supreme knowledge? It should be said, the five appropriable khandhas. That is: the form appropriable khandha, the feeling appropriable khandha, the perception appropriable khandha, the sankharas appropriable khandha, the consciousness appropriable khandha. These are the dhammas that should be comprehensively known by supreme knowledge.
–MN 149

Same is said about the five remaining faculties.

When the ariya abides knowing and seeing the eye, forms, eye consciousness, eye contact and the feeling born of eye contact as they have come to be, this is an abiding in an immeasurable samadhi where measurable kamma accumulated in past lives cannot endure, and have to be felt to bring about their extinction as explained in the loṇaphala sutta (AN 3.100). As the Buddha makes it clear, one’s thirsting also goes to extinction. With the extinction of thirsting, all previous accumulated kamma also goes to extinction as described in the khaya sutta (SN 46.26).

“Here, O Udayi, a bhikkhu enculturates the mindfulness awakening factor in dependence on seclusion, in dependence on fading away, in dependence on cessation, maturing in relinquishment, abundant, exalted, immeasurable, free from affliction. For him enculturating the mindfulness awakening factor in dependence on seclusion, in dependence on fading away, in dependence on cessation, maturing in relinquishment, abundant, exalted, immeasurable, free from affliction, there is the abandoning of thirsting. With the abandoning of thirsting, there is the abandoning of kamma. With the abandoning of kamma, there is the abandoning of suffering. Thus, O Udayi, from the extinction of thirsting comes the extinction of kamma. From the extinction of kamma comes the extinction of suffering.”
–SN 46.26

Same is said about the six remaining awakening factors.

In the nigaṇṭha sutta (AN 3.74), the venerable Ānanda explains to a disciple of the Niganthas the proper way of wearing away past kamma in the Noble One’s discipline as follows:

There are these three wearing away purifications, O Abhaya, rightly declared by the Auspicious One who knows and sees, the arahant, the rightly self-awakened one, for the purification of beings, for the surmounting of sorrow and lamentation, for the subsiding of mental and physical suffering, for the attainment of the know-how, for the realization of nibbana.

What three? “Here, O Abhaya, a bhikkhu is virtuous. He dwells restrained by the restraint of the patimokkha, endowed with conduct and resort, seeing danger in the slightest faults. Having undertaken the training rules, he trains in them. He does not do any new kamma and eliminates the old kamma touch after touch. The wearing away is visible here and now, not involving time, inviting eye witness, leading onwards, to be personally experienced by the wise.”

That bhikkhu, O Abhaya, thus endowed with virtue, quite secluded from sense-desires, secluded from unwholesome dhammas, he enters upon and abides in the pleasure with happiness born of seclusion, with thinking and with pondering, the first jhana. From the subsiding of thinking and pondering, with the internal tranquilization and unification of mind, without thinking and pondering, he enters upon and abides in the pleasure with happiness born of samādhi, the second jhana. With the fading away of happiness, dwelling mindful and contemplative, he looks on with equanimity as he experiences pleasure in the body, of which the Noble Ones say: “The mindful one is he who dwells in pleasure with equanimity.” He enters upon and abides in the third jhana. From the abandoning of [physical] pleasure and pain, from the extinction of former mental pleasure and mental pain, he enters upon and abides in the utter purity of mindfulness and equanimity without pain, without pleasure, the fourth jhana. He does not do any new kamma and eliminates the old kamma touch after touch. The wearing away is visible here and now, not involving time, inviting eye witness, leading onwards, to be personally experienced by the wise.”

That bhikkhu, O Abhaya, thus endowed with virtue, thus endowed with samadhi, with the extinction of the asavas, having realized for himself with supreme knowledge, in this very life, the asava-free mind liberation, wisdom liberation, and having entered upon it, dwells in it. He does not do any new kamma and eliminates the old kamma touch after touch. The wearing away is visible here and now, not involving time, inviting eye witness, leading onwards, to be personally experienced by the wise.”

These are the three wearing away purifications, O Abhaya, rightly declared by the Auspicious One who knows and sees, the arahant, the rightly self-awakened one, for the purification of beings, for the surmounting of sorrow and lamentation, for the subsiding of mental and physical suffering, for the attainment of the know-how, for the realization of nibbana.

Note that old kamma has a double meaning in this sutta. Since the eye, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind are defined as old kamma by the Buddha, bringing about the cessation of any of them is said to be the wearing away of old kamma by touching cessation with it without interval (ānantarika samādhi), touch after touch. It is also said to be the wearing away of old accumulated kamma since this is an abiding in an immeasurable samādhi where measurable accumulated kamma cannot endure.

The bhikkhu endowed with virtue is a stream enterer or a once-returner (see kīṭāgiri sutta MN 70), who abides in an immeasurable samādhi experiencing the attainment of knowledge and vision by touching cessation with the eye ( dhamma cakkhu). Since no new kamma can be created with intention for forms as cause because the eye has ceased and perception of forms have faded away, thus it is said: “he does not create new kamma”. Since the eye is terminated by cessation and the eye is old kamma, it is said: “he terminates old kamma.” Because he continuously touches cessation with the eye without interval ānantarika samādhi, it is said: “touch after touch.” Because the eye has been brought to cessation, old kamma is said to have worn away. This wearing away is visible here and now, not involving time, inviting eye witness, leading onwards, to be personally experienced by the wise.”

The bhikkhu endowed with samādhi is a non-returner (see kīṭāgiri sutta MN 70), who abides in an immeasurable samādhi using the jhanas (and possibly the four brahma viharas or the domain of infinite space, the domain of infinite consciousness, or the domain of nothingness) as the gateway for touching cessation with the body and mind (see aṭṭhakanāgara sutta, AN 11.16). Since no new kamma can be created with bodily intention, verbal intention or mental intention as cause because the body and mind have ceased, it is said: “he does not create new kamma.” Since the body and mind are terminated by cessation and the body and mind are old kamma, it is said: “he terminates old kamma.” Because he continuously touches cessation with the body and mind without interval ānantarika samādhi, it is said: “touch after touch.” Because the body and mind have been brought to cessation, old kamma is said to have worn away. This wearing away is visible here and now, not involving time, inviting eye witness, leading onwards, to be personally experienced by the wise.”

The bhikkhu who, with the extinction of the asavas, having realized for himself with supreme knowledge, in this very life, the asava-free mind liberation, wisdom liberation, and having entered upon it, dwells in it is the arahant, who abides in an immeasurable samādhi where all the six sense domain ceases without remainder. Since the arahant is incapable of creating new kamma, it is said: “he does not create new kamma.” Since all six senses are terminated by cessation and the six senses are old kamma, it is said: “he terminates old kamma.” Because he continuously touches cessation with the six senses without interval ānantarika samādhi, it is said: “touch after touch.” Because the six senses have been brought to cessation, old kamma is said to have worn away. This wearing away is visible here and now, not involving time, inviting eye witness, leading onwards, to be personally experienced by the wise.”

Cessation of New Kamma

Now to answer question 2. What about the results of the intentional kamma accumulated in the present life? Does the arahant have to experience all of the intentional kamma accumulated in the present life as well or can the arahant escape their results?

As we have noted from the nidāna sutta (AN 3.34), all the intentional kamma accumulated with non-greed, non-hatred and non-delusion as source get abandoned when greed, hatred and delusion are eliminated. The Buddha gives a very illustrative simile as follows:

Just as, O bhikkhus, that there are seeds that are unbroken, unspoiled, undamaged by wind and sun, fertile, well kept. A man would burn them in a fire, having burnt it in a fire, he would make them ashes. having made them ashes, he would winnow them in a strong wind or he would cause them to be carried away in a swift flowing river. Thus, O bhikkhus, those seeds would be cut off at the root, made like palm stump, obliterated so that they are no longer subject to future arising.

So too, O bhikkhus, whatever kamma, is fashioned by non-greed, non-hatred or non-delusion, born of non-greed, non-hatred or non-delusion, with non-greed, non-hatred or non-delusion as its source, with non-greed, non-hatred or non-delusion as its origin, that kamma is abandoned when greed, hatred and delusion are eliminated. It is cut off at the root, made like palm stump, obliterated so that it is no longer subject to future arising.
–AN 3.34

This type of kamma is also described as the neither dark nor bright kamma with neither dark nor bright results which leads to the cessation of kamma (see kamma sutta AN 4.233). So once it serves its purpose of eliminating greed, hatred and delusion, which is also the cessation of kamma, then it is no longer needed and gets abandoned, since it neither leads to dark nor bright results.

In brief, the debt of accumulated kamma, whether born of greed, hatred and delusion or born of non-greed, non-hatred and non-delusion, are only paid off by experiencing their results. Since the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion is attained in exchange for the destruction of accumulated kamma born of non-greed, non-hatred and non-delusion, only the debt of kamma accumulated in the present life born of greed, hatred and delusion remains to be paid off by experiencing its results for the arahant. As the dhammapada verses 127 states, ”a place in the world does not exists, where staying one could be released from evil kamma”, not even an arahant. The loṇaphala sutta (AN 3.100) explains how all unwholesome kamma accumulated in the present life come to be experienced in this very life by the arahant without any residue remaining as follows:

Whoever, O bhikkhus, might say thus: “In which ever way that a person creates a kamma, in just that way he experiences.” That being so, O bhikkhus, there is not the living the life of purity and an opportunity for rightly making an end of suffering is not evident.

But whoever, O bhikkhus, might say thus: “In which ever way to be felt that a person creates a kamma, in just that way he feels the results.” That being so, O bhikkhus, there is the living the life of purity and an opportunity for rightly making an end of suffering is evident.

Here, O bhikkhus, for a certain person, even a trifling measure of evil kamma done leads him to hell. However here, O bhikkhus, for a certain person, that same trifling measure of evil kamma done is to be felt in this life without even a tiny [residual] to be seen, what [to speak of] very much [residual].

For what kind of person, O bhikkhus, does even a trifling measure of evil kamma done lead him to hell?

Here, O bhikkhus, a certain person is undeveloped in body, undeveloped in virtue, undeveloped in mind, and undeveloped in wisdom. He is inconsequential, depraved, and dwells in suffering over insignificant [matters]. For this kind of person, O bhikkhus, even a trifling measure of evil kamma done lead him to hell?

For what kind of person, O bhikkhus, does that same trifling measure of evil kamma done is to be felt in this life without even a tiny [residual] to be seen, what [to speak of] very much [residual].

Here, O bhikkhus, a certain person is developed in body, developed in virtue, developed in mind, and developed in wisdom. He is influential, magnanimous, and dwells in immeasurable [samadhi]. For this kind of person, that same trifling measure of evil kamma done is to be felt in this life without even a tiny [residual] to be seen, what [to speak of] very much [residual].

“Just as, O bhikkhus, a salt crystal were to be dropped in a small cup of water. So what do you think, O bhikkhus, would such a salt crystal make the small cup of water salty and undrinkable?”

Yes, Bhante.

What is the reason for that?

Because, O Bhante, however much water there is in such a small cup, such a salt crystal would make that water salty and undrinkable.”

“Just as, O bhikkhus, a salt crystal were to be dropped in the river Ganges. So what do you think, O bhikkhus, would such a salt crystal make the river Ganges salty and undrinkable?”

Certainly not, Bhante.

What is the reason for that?

“Because, O Bhante, the river Ganges is an expansive body of water, such a salt crystal would certainly not make that water salty and undrinkable.”

So too, O bhikkhus, here for a certain person, even a trifling measure of evil kamma done leads him to hell. However here, O bhikkhus, for a certain person, that same trifling measure of evil kamma done is to be felt in this life without even a tiny [residual] to be seen, what [to speak of] very much [residual].
–AN 3.100

The person who is developed in body, developed in virtue, developed in mind, and developed in wisdom is the arahant. However, as explained in the nigaṇṭha sutta (AN 3.74) the one accomplished in virtue (a stream enterer or once-returner) and the one accomplished in samādhi (the non-returner) also have access to an immeasurable samādhi that depletes accumulated kamma.

This is similar to the process of eliminating kamma accumulated in the present life by the ariya disciple entering upon and abiding in a mundane immeasurable abiding such as the metta liberation of mind (mettācetovimutti) or any of the other brahmavihara attainments where measurable kamma accumulated in the present life cannot endure and have to be felt and brought to extinction as in the sankhadama sutta (SN 42.8):

The noble disciple, O village chief, thus rid of covetousness, rid of ill-will, unconfused, mindful and contemplative, with a mind imbued with metta, dwells pervading one direction, likewise the second direction, likewise the third direction, likewise the fourth direction, above, below, around, to all beings in the entire world he dwells pervading with a mind imbued with metta, abundant, exalted, immeasurable, and free from enmity and ill-will. Just as, O village chief, a powerful conch shell blower would easily, without difficulty, convey [the sound of his conch shell] in the four directions, just so, O village chief, with the liberation of mind by metta thus enculturated, thus made abundant, any measureable kamma done will not remain there, will not persist there.
–SN 42.8

A good example of the inescapability of evil kamma done in the present life is given in the anguḷimāla sutta (MN 86). Angulamala had killed many people prior to his encounter with the Buddha. He acquired confidence in the Buddha upon meeting him and soon after gaining bhikkhu ordination under the Buddha, he attained the noble birth of stream entry. The following is an account of the venerable Angulimala as an arahant experiencing the results of having killed many people in this life:

Then in the morning, the venerable Angulimala, having dressed, having taken bowl and outer robe, entered Savatthi for alms food. At that time, some threw stones that landed on the venerable Angulimala’s body, others threw sticks that landed on the venerable Angulimala’s body, others also threw potsherd that landed on the venerable Angulimala’s body. So then the venerable Angulimala, with a broken bowl, with his head cut with blood dripping, with his outer robe torn apart, visited with the Auspicious One. The Auspicious One saw the venerable Angulimala approaching from afar. Having seen, he said this to the venerable Angulimala: “You bear it, O Brahmin. You bear it, O Brahmin. You, O Brahmin, are experiencing the results of kamma in this very life which, were it to ripen in hell, you would have roasted for many years, for many hundreds of years, for many thousands of years, for many hundreds of thousands of years.”
–MN 86

Once all the present life accumulated unwholesome kamma are also fully experienced, then the arahant is entirely free from all accumulated kamma, past or present (kammanirodha), and is said to be ownerless (nirupadhī), one who eats his food free from debt (anaṇo bhuñjati bhojanaṁ) as explained in the anguḷimāla sutta verses (MN 86):

tādisaṃ kammaṃ katvāna,

phuṭṭho kammavipākena,

Having done much kamma [in this life],
of the kind leading to the lower worlds;
the results of that kamma has reached me now,
and so I eat my food free from debt.
–MN 86

He is a Sambuddha, O brahmin, a transcender of all dhammas,
having attained all higher knowledges and powers,
endowed with Eye in all dhammas;
he has attained to the extinction of all kamma,
liberated through the extinction of all ownership.
–Snp 5.0

Exhausted is old [kamma], there is no new origination,
the mind is untainted in regard to future becoming;
the seeds extincted without desire for growth,

–Snp 2.1

the one divested of all kamma;

even the devas praise him,
by Brahma too, he is praised.
–AN 4.28

Difference from the Niganthas?

An obvious question may be raised that if the depletion of the intentional kamma accumulated in past lives is such a gating item for the attainment of arahantship, why doesn’t the Buddha make the depletion of kamma the purpose of his teaching for bringing about the ending of suffering, similar to the Niganthas? On the contrary, we learn from the mahakotthita sutta (AN 9.13) that manipulation of kamma to wear them away is not the purpose of the life of purity under the Auspicious One:

Then the venerable Mahakotthita visited with the venerable Sariputta. Upon arrival, he exchanged friendly greetings with the venerable Sariputta. Having exchanged friendly greetings and cordial talk, he sat down to one side. Having sat down to one side, the venerable Mahakotthita said this to the venerable Sariputta.

How about this, O friend, Sariputta, “Let whichever kamma is to be experienced in this life be experienced by me in the after-life.” Is this the purpose for which the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?

Certainly not, O friend.

Then how about this, O friend, Sariputta, “Let whichever kamma is to be experienced in the after-life be experienced by me in this life.” Is this the purpose for which the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?

Certainly not, O friend.

How about this, O friend, Sariputta, “Let whichever kamma is to be experienced as pleasant be experienced by me as painful.” Is this the purpose for which the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?

Certainly not, O friend.

Then how about this, O friend, Sariputta, “Let whichever kamma is to be experienced as painful be experienced by me as pleasant.” Is this the purpose for which the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?

Certainly not, O friend.

How about this, O friend, Sariputta, “Let whichever kamma is to be experienced when ripened be experienced by me when unripened.” Is this the purpose for which the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?

Certainly not, O friend.

Then how about this, O friend, Sariputta, “Let whichever kamma is to be experienced when unripened be experienced by me when ripened.” Is this the purpose for which the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?

Certainly not, O friend.

How about this, O friend, Sariputta, “Let whichever kamma is to be experienced substantially be experienced by me slightly.” Is this the purpose for which the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?

Certainly not, O friend.

Then how about this, O friend, Sariputta, “Let whichever kamma is to be experienced slightly be experienced by me substantially.” Is this the purpose for which the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?

Certainly not, O friend.

How about this, O friend, Sariputta, “Let whichever kamma is to be experienced not be experienced by me.” Is this the purpose for which the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?

Certainly not, O friend.

Then how about this, O friend, Sariputta, “Let whichever kamma is not to be experienced be experienced by me.” Is this the purpose for which the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?

Certainly not, O friend.

“Now then, for what purpose, O friend, is the life of purity lived under the Auspicious One?”

“Indeed, O friend, it is for the knowledge, for the vision, for the attainment, for the realization, for the breakthrough, of that which is unknown, unseen, unattained, unrealized, unbroken through, that the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One.”

“But what, O friend, is unknown, unseen, unattained, unrealized, unbroken through, for the sake of its knowledge, vision, attainment, realization, and breakthrough that the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One?”

“This is suffering”, O friend, is unknown, unseen, unattained, unrealized, unbroken through. It is for the sake of its knowledge, vision, attainment, realization, and breakthrough that the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One.” “This is the origination of suffering”, O friend, is unknown, unseen, unattained, unrealized, unbroken through. It is for the sake of its knowledge, vision, attainment, realization, and breakthrough that the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One.” “This is the path leading to the cessation of suffering”, O friend, is unknown, unseen, unattained, unrealized, unbroken through. It is for the sake of its knowledge, vision, attainment, realization, and breakthrough that the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One.”

Indeed, O friend, it is for the knowledge, for the vision, for the attainment, for the realization, for the breakthrough of this which is unknown, unseen, unattained, unrealized, unbroken through, that the life of purity is lived under the Auspicious One.”
–AN 9.13

The reason why the Buddha does not single out the depletion of kamma as the solution to suffering like the Niganthas is because kamma is cetana and cetana is only a subset of the sankhara appropriable khandha. So even if one were to wear away all kamma of the past lives in order to be free from their results and not create new kamma, one would still not be freed from suffering since the Buddha declares that all five appropriable khandhas are suffering, not just kamma. In the absence of all new and old kamma, there will remain form, feeling, perception, other types of sankharas, and consciousness which are also suffering. In the nibbedhika sutta (AN 6.63), the Buddha includes kamma as only one of the six dhammas whose cessation is to be known as the penetrative life of purity. Also in the sivaka sutta (SN 36.21), the Buddha gives eight causes of afflictions, with the results of kamma being only one of them. And so even if one could deplete all old kamma, one would still be subject to afflictions from the other seven causes of affliction. Thus the depletion of old kamma is only a necessary part of the solution but not sufficient to bring about the ending of suffering, and so the Buddha rather places emphasis on the depletion of all five appropriable khandhas which he defines as suffering in the four noble truths in order to bring about an ending of suffering. In other words, the depletion of old kamma, being a wholesome dhamma, is already included in the four noble truths so need not to be singled out as stated by the venerable Sariputta in the mahāhatthipadopama sutta (MN 28):

Just as, O friends, of whatever living beings that walk, the footprint of any of them fits within an elephants footprint, and the elephants foot is declared the foremost among them with respect to its largeness, in the same way, O friends, whatever wholesome dhammas there are, all of them are included in the four noble truths.
–MN 28

Designating the Arahant’s Actions

The arahants are said to possess the following knowledge: “Done is what is to be done (kataṃ karaṇīyaṃ); there is nothing further to be done (natthi kiñci uttari karaṇīyaṃ); there is no addition of what is done (natthi katassa paṭicayo)”. So then the question arises as to how should the actions of the arahant be designated.

The proponents of ahosikamma suggest the following:

The actions of Arahants can be called ”kamma” or “kiriya” (which are synonyms), without implying that Arahants accumulate kammic potential that leads to kammic results (kammavipāka) in the future. All six roots (mūla) are gone, yet their actions (kamma/kiriya) are free from greed (lobha), hatred (dosa) and delusion (moha) and therefore non-greedy (alobha), non-hating (adosa) and undeluded (amoha).

Their actions certainly have effects on themselves and others, but these effects are not governed by the law of kammavipāka and its three (?? 2 or repeating for long- if wishing so) times of ripening (during present, next or a future life).

Their actions (kamma/kiriya) are performed through body, speech or mind, yet they don ́t accumulate kamma that can produce results (vipāka) in the future.

However, in the kammanirodha sutta (SN 35.146) the Buddha designates the actions of an arahants as kammanirodha.The Buddha’s own designation of the activities of the arahant as kammanirodha, but the proponents of ahosikamma have brought forth few arguments as to justify their designation of the activities of the arahant as kamma or kiriya. The rest of this article points out the problems inherent in their arguments in an attempt to provide a more convincing reason for using the Buddha’s own designation of “kammanirodha” to designate the actions of arahants.

The arguments they use to justify the kamma-of-arahants view are analyzed in the remainder of this article in order to show the following:

  1. The arahant’s actions cannot be designated as kamma without contradicting the four noble truths.

  2. The arahant’s actions cannot be designated as kamma without proliferating that which ought not be proliferated, an unwarranted papañcā.

  3. The arahant’s actions cannot be designated as kamma without encountering a self-contradiction.

Repudiation of the kamma-of-arahants view by way of exposing a contradiction to the four noble truths

To justify the kamma-of-arahants view, the proponents of defunctable kamma argue:

“The relationship between sammā kammantā and kammanirodha is understood as follows: Sammā kammantā, when supported by the other path factors, eventually leads (saṁvattati) to the cessation of rebirth-productive kamma at the time of attainment of Arahantship, and to the cessation of all actions (kamma) by body speech or mind, at the time of the Arahant’s khandhaparinibbāna.”

This introduces an abhhidhamma style re-definition of kammanirodha as rebirth-productive kammanirodha upon the attainment of arahantship, and all-action (by body, speech or mind) kammanirodha at the time of the Arahant’s khandhaparinibbāna. The term “khandhaparinibbāna” is not found in the suttas. Neither is there any distinction in the suttas between nibbana at the time of arahantship attainment (sandiṭṭhikanibbāna), and parinibbāna (see AN 9.47-49). Since this term seems to refer to the arahant’s [final] “cessation of the six sense bases of contact”, this phrasing is used instead, to make it easier for those who are not familiar with abhidhamma or commentarial terms. Let us analyze this abhidhammic re-definition of kammanirodha from the perspective of the kammanirodha sutta to see if it is justified.

The kammanirodha sutta (SN 35.146) is as follows:

“O bhikkhus, I will teach you new and old kamma, the cessation of kamma, and the way leading to the cessation of kamma. Listen to that and do mind it well, I will speak.”

And what, O bhikkhus, is old kamma? The eye, O bhikkhus, is old kamma, especially conditioned, especially intended, to be felt, to be seen. …pe…. The mind, O bhikkhus, is old kamma, especially conditioned, especially intended, to be felt, to be seen. This is called, O bhikkhus, old kamma.

And what, O bhikkhus, is new kamma? O bhikkhus, whatever present action is done by body, speech, or mind. This is called, O bhikkhus, new kamma.

And what, O bhikkhus, is the cessation of kamma? [That] which touches liberation through the cessation of bodily action, verbal action and mental action. This is called, O bhikkhus, the cessation of kamma.

“And what, O bhikkhus, is the path leading to the cessation of kamma? It is just this noble eightfold path, that is – right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood , right effort, right mindfulness, right samādhi. This is called, O bhikkhus, the path leading to the cessation of kamma.

“Thus, O bhikkhus, I have taught old kamma, I have taught new kamma, I have taught the cessation of kamma, I have taught the way leading to the cessation of kamma. Whatever should be done, O bhikkhus, by a teacher for his disciples, desiring their welfare, having pity for them, that has been done for you by me, out of compassion. These are the feet of trees, O bhikkhus, these are empty huts. Cultivate jhāna, O bhikkhus, do not be negligent, lest you regret it later. This is our instruction to you.”

The kammanirodha sutta is a formulation of the four noble truths in terms of kamma instead of dukkha as expressed in the standard formulation.

To show that the kamma formulation is equivalent to the standard formulation, it is shown that each noble truth in the standard formulation is equivalent to the corresponding noble truth in the kamma formulation as follows:

The standard formulation identifies the five holding aggregates as suffering in brief (saṃkhittena pañcupādānakkhandhā dukkhā), indicating that the six sense bases (old kamma) must be identified as suffering in the kamma formulation. Let’s see if that is the case. From the mahāhatthipadopama sutta (MN 28), we know that each sense base of contact arises with the five holding aggregates. Thus, the arising of the sense bases of contact is the arising of suffering. Also in the nandi sutta (SN5.19) it is said:

One who seeks delight in the eye, O bhikkhus, seeks delight in suffering. One who seeks delight in suffering is not freed from suffering, I say. One who seeks delight in the ear… nose… tongue… body… mind seeks delight in suffering. One who seeks delight in suffering is not freed from suffering, I say.

So we see an agreement between the standard formulation and kamma formulation of the first noble truth.

The second noble truth in the standard formulation defines thirsting as the origination of suffering, whereas the kamma formulation defines “whatever present action is done by body, speech, or mind” (new kamma) as the origination of suffering. This implies that the arising of present bodily, verbal or mental action is the arising of thirsting. This is consistent with the Buddha’s designation of kamma in the paṭhamanidāna sutta (AN 3.111) as either leading to the origination of kamma or to the cessation of kamma, which is applicable only to those who have not destroyed thirsting. Thus, we see an agreement between the standard formulation and kamma formulation of the second noble truth.

The third noble truth in the standard formulation defines the extinction of thirsting as the cessation of suffering, whereas the third noble truth in the kamma formulation defines “[that] which touches liberation through the cessation of bodily action, verbal action and mental action” (kammanirodha) as the cessation of suffering. In the suttas, we find that the extinction of kamma is synonymous with the extinction of thirsting. For example, here is a verse from the pārāyanavagga (Snp 5.0) by a deva extolling the Buddha’s extinction of all kamma.

“so hi brāhmaṇa sambuddho, sabbadhammāna pāragū. sabbābhiññābalappatto, sabbadhammesu cakkhumā. sabbakammakkhayaṃ patto, vimutto upadhikkhaye.

He is a Sambuddha, brahmin, a transcender of all dhammas, Having attained all higher knowledges and powers, endowed with eye in all dhammas, he has attained to the extinction of all kamma, liberated through the extinction of all ownership.

And here is a verse where the Buddha himself extolls the arahant’s abandonment of all kamma in the mahaviyuha sutta (Snp 4.13).

Having abandoned all virtue and observances and kamma, whether blameworthy or blameless, with no aspiration regarding “the pure and the impure”, he would live unconcerned, not grasping after peace.

So from these examples, the extinction of thirsting is the destruction of kamma. Thus, we see an agreement between the standard formulation and kamma formulation of the third noble truth.

The agreement between the standard formulation and the kamma formulation for the forth noble truth is self-explanatory and need no further elaboration.

Thus, the kammanirodha sutta can be summarize as follows:

  1. The eye, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind bases of contact (old kamma) is suffering. It must be fully comprehended.

  2. Whatever present action is done by body, speech, or mind (new kamma) is the origination of suffering. It must be abandoned.

  3. Attainment of liberation through the cessation of bodily action, verbal action and mental action (kammanirodha) is the cessation of suffering. It must be realized.

  4. Just this noble eightfold path is the path leading to the cessation of suffering (kamma). It must be enculturated.

So we see that the kammanorodha sutta is a deep exposition of the four noble truths.

If we go by the abhidhammic re-definition of kammanirodha that the arahant only realizes half of kammanirodha upon attaining arahantship and the other half at the time of the arahant’s [final] cessation of the six sense bases of contact, we have to conclude, for the sake of consistency with the four noble truths, that the arahant only realizes half of the cessation of suffering upon attaining arahantship and the other half at the time of the arahant’s [final] cessation of the six sense bases of contact. But this is a grave contradiction of the four noble truths as taught by the Buddha. Thus, we see that designating the arahant’s action as kamma by re-defining kammanirodha contradicts the Buddha’s teaching of the four noble truths.

Repudiation of the kamma-of-arahants” view by way of exposing unwarranted papañcā

Let’s subject the following argument to further scrutiny in light of the suttas to show that it is unwarranted papañcā.

“Sammā kammantā, when supported by the other path factors, eventually leads (saṁvattati) to the cessation of rebirth-productive kamma at the time of attainment of Arahantship, and to the cessation of all actions (kamma) by body, speech or mind, at the time of the Arahant’s khandhaparinibbāna.”

From the sekha sutta (SN 48.53), the Buddha explains one beyond training as follows:

Puna caparaṃ, bhikkhave, asekho bhikkhucha indriyāni pajānāti. ’Cakkhundriyaṃ, sotindriyaṃ, ghānindriyaṃ, jivhindriyaṃ, kāyindriyaṃ, manindriyaṃ—’imāni kho cha indriyāni sabbena sabbaṃsabbathā sabbaṃ aparisesaṃ nirujjhissanti, aññāni ca cha indriyāni na kuhiñci kismiñciuppajjissantī’ti pajānāti. Ayampi kho, bhikkhave, pariyāyo yaṃpariyāyaṃ āgamma asekho bhikkhuasekhabhūmiyaṃ ṭhito ’asekhosmī’ti pajānātī”ti.

Again, bhikkhus, a bhikkhu who is one beyond training wisely understands the six faculties—the eye faculty, the ear faculty, the nose faculty, the tongue faculty, the body faculty, the mind faculty. He wisely understands: ‘These six faculties will cease completely and totally without remainder, and no other six faculties will arise anywhere in any way.’ This too is a method by means of which a bhikkhu who is beyond training, standing on the plane of one beyond training, wisely understands: ‘I am one beyond training.’

So we see that the time of the arahant’s exhaustion of life, the breakup of the body, is the remainderless fading away and cessation of the six bases of contact. The kotthita sutta (AN 4.173) makes it clear that any speculations with reference to the cessation of the six sense bases of contact is unwarranted papañcā.

“ ’channaṃ, āvuso, phassāyatanānaṃ asesavirāganirodhā atthaññaṃ kiñcī’ti, iti vadaṃ appapañcaṃ papañceti. ’channaṃ, āvuso, phassāyatanānaṃ asesavirāganirodhā natthaññaṃ kiñcī’ti, iti vadaṃ appapañcaṃ papañceti. ’channaṃ, āvuso, phassāyatanānaṃ asesavirāganirodhā atthi ca natthi ca aññaṃ kiñcī’ti, iti vadaṃ appapañcaṃ papañceti. ’channaṃ, āvuso, phassāyatanānaṃ asesavirāganirodhā nevatthi no natthaññaṃ kiñcī’ti, iti vadaṃ appapañcaṃ papañceti.

“Friend, if one says thus: ‘With the remainderless fading away and cessation of the six bases of contact, there is something else’, one proliferates that which ought not be proliferated. Friend, if one says thus: ‘With the remainderless fading away and cessation of the six bases of contact, there is nothing else’, one proliferates that which ought not be proliferated. Friend if one says thus: ‘With the remainderless fading away and cessation of the six bases of contact, there is both something else and nothing else’, one proliferates that which ought not be proliferated. Friend, if one says thus: ‘With the remainderless fading away and cessation of the six bases of contact, there is neither something else nor nothing else,’ one proliferates that which ought not be proliferated.”

yāvatā, āvuso, channaṃ phassāyatanānaṃ gati tāvatā papañcassa gati; yāvatā papañcassa gati tāvatā channaṃ phassāyatanānaṃ gati. channaṃ, āvuso, phassāyatanānaṃ asesavirāganirodhā papañcanirodho papañcavūpasamo”ti.

Friend, as far as the range of the six bases of contact extends, just so far extends the range of papañcā. As far as the range of papañcā extends, just so far extends the range of the six bases for contact. With the remainderless fading away and cessation of the six bases of contact there is the cessation of papañcā, the subsiding of papañcā.”

From this sutta, we see that even the very notion that the arahant has some all actions (kamma) by body speech or mind (a something else) left to be abandoned at the time of the [final] cessation of the six bases of contact, or half a kammanirodha to be attained at the time of the [final] cessation of the six bases of contact, is a proliferation of that which ought not be proliferated, an unwarranted papañcā.

Given the qualities of the well proclaimed Dhamma as: ’The Dhamma is well proclaimed by the Auspicious One (svākkhāto bhagavatā dhammo), directly visible [in this very life] (sandiṭṭhiko), not involving time (akāliko), [inviting one to] come and see (ehipassiko), leading onwards (opanāyiko), to be personally experienced by the wise (paccattaṃ veditabbo viññūhī), the arahant need not wait for the breakup of the body before attaining the cessation of all action. So we see that there is no justification for re-defining the Buddha’s definition of kammanirodha to fit the kamma-of-arahant thesis.

Repudiation of the kamma-of-arahants view by way of exposing a self-contradiction

The following analysis will show that the arahant’s actions cannot be designated as kamma without encountering a self-contradiction.

Now let’s analyze the following argument for justifying the kamma-of-arahants view:

“When he says “Whatever action one does now by body, speech, or mind. This is called new kamma.”, this refers to kamma in its active/productive form, as the action that leads to results (vipāka) for the doer in the future.”

Here again, there is an abhidhammic style re-definition of “new kamma”. New kamma is re-defined as active/productive kamma that leads to results (vipāka) for the doer in the future, implying that there exists non-active/non-productive kamma that does not lead to results (vipāka) for the doer in the future.

To clarify the distinction between the Buddha’s definition of “new kamma” and the abhidhammic re-definition, Here is the relevant section of the khammanirodha sutta (SN 35.146):

katamañca, bhikkhave, navakammaṃ? yaṃ kho, bhikkhave, etarahi kammaṃ karoti kāyena vācāya manasā, idaṃ vuccati, bhikkhave, navakammaṃ.

And what, O bhikkhus, is new kamma? O bhikkhus, whatever present action is done by body, speech, or mind. This is called, O bhikkhus, new kamma.

From this translation, we get a very clear and concise definition of “new kamma” from the Buddha as whatever present action is done by body, speech, or mind.

Now, let’s analyze the abhidhammic re-definition of “new kamma” to show that the arahant’s actions cannot be designated as kamma without encountering a self-contradiction.

In the nibbedhika Sutta (AN 6.63), the Buddha defines kamma as cetanā, saying “It is volition (cetanā), O bhikkhus, that I call kamma. For having intended, one acts by body, speech, or mind”.

In the upādānaparipavatta sutta (SN 22.56), the Budda defines saṅkhārā in panca upādānakhanda as cetanā:

“katame ca, bhikkhave, saṅkhārā? chayime, bhikkhave, cetanākāyā — rūpasañcetanā, saddasañcetanā, gandhasañcetanā, rasasañcetanā, phoṭṭhabbasañcetanā, dhammasañcetanā. ime vuccanti, bhikkhave, saṅkhārā.

“And what, O bhikkhus, are saṅkhārā? There are these six classes of volition — volition regarding forms, volition regarding sounds, volition regarding odours, volition regarding tastes, volition regarding tangibles, volition regarding dhammas. These, O bhikkhus, are called sankhārā.

In the khajjanīya sutta (SN 22.79), the Buddha explains why saṅkhārā are called saṅkhārā as follows:

Kiñca, bhikkhave, saṅkhāre vadetha Saṅkhatamabhisaṅkharontīti kho, bhikkhave, tasmā ’saṅkhārā’ti vuccati.

Kiñca saṅkhatamabhisaṅkharonti? Rūpaṃ rūpattāya saṅkhatamabhisaṅkharonti, vedanaṃ vedanattāya saṅkhatamabhisaṅkharonti, saññaṃ saññattāya saṅkhatamabhisaṅkharonti, saṅkhāre saṅkhārattāya saṅkhatamabhisaṅkharonti, viññāṇaṃ viññāṇattāya saṅkhatamabhisaṅkharonti. Saṅkhatamabhisaṅkharontīti kho, bhikkhave, tasmā ’saṅkhārā’ti vuccati.

And why, O bhikkhus, do you call them saṅkhārā? ‘They precondition the conditioned,’ O bhikkhus, therefore they are called saṅkhārā.

And what is the conditioned that they precondition? ‘They precondition conditioned form as form; they precondition conditioned feeling as feeling; they precondition conditioned perception as perception; they precondition conditioned saṅkhārā as saṅkhārā; they precondition conditioned consciousness as consciousness’. ‘They precondition the conditioned,’ O bhikkhus, therefore they are called saṅkhārā.

From this definition, since saṅkhārā precondition saṅkhārā upādānakkhandhā themselves, we see that saṅkhārā leads to origination of saṅkhārā. But what about the other four saṅkhatā that saṅkhārā preconditions (form, feeling, perception and consciousness)? These also being upādānakkhandhā, we know they are anicca, saṅkhata, paṭicca samuppannaṃ, and since all saṅkhārā are anicca (sabbe saṅkhārā anicca), these four saṅkhatā are also saṅkhārā, thus we can conclude that saṅkhārā lead to origination of saṅkhārā.

But do there exist saṅkhārā that lead to the cessation of saṅkhārā? From aggappasādā sutta (AN 4.34), the Buddha declares:

yāvatā, bhikkhave, dhammā saṅkhatā, ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo tesaṃ aggamakkhāyati.

“To whatever extent there are dhammas that are conditioned, the noble eightfold path is declared the foremost among them”.

We know that the noble eightfold path lead to stilling of all saṅkhārā (sabbasaṅkhārasamatho), and since the noble eightfold path is saṅkhatā, and saṅkhatā are saṅkhārā, thus saṅkhārā also lead to the cessation of saṅkhārā.

So we can conclude that, saṅkhārā either lead to the origination of saṅkhārā or to the cessation of saṅkhārā.

The question may arise as to whether there could be saṅkhārā that neither lead to origination of saṅkhārā nor to the cessation of saṅkhārā. The answer is that there cannot be, as such a saṅkhārā will be asaṅkhata (nibbāna), a self-contradiction. A “black is white” principle.

Thus, if kamma is cetanā and cetanā is a type of saṅkhārā, and saṅkhārā has only two possibilities, either leading to the origination of saṅkhārā or to the cessation of saṅkhārā, then we must conclude that kamma, being a type of saṅkhārā can only have two possibilities as well, either leading to the origination of kamma or to the cessation of kamma. No other possibilities can exist. So far, so good, but the analysis to reach this conclusion may appear too technical, so let’s see if this conclusion that kamma either leads to the origination of kamma or to the cessation of kamma has any backing in the suttas.

From the paṭhamanidāna sutta (AN 3.111), we know that any action born of greed, hatred or delusion is designated as kamma that leads to the origination of kamma (kammaṃ kammasamudayāya saṃvattati), whereas any action born of non-greed, non-hatred or non-delusion is designated as kamma that leads to the cessation of kamma (kammaṃ kammanirodhāya saṃvattati).

So from this sutta, we find only two possibilities for kamma as well: kamma either leads to the origination of kamma or leads to the cessation of kamma. A non-active/non-productive kamma that neither leads to origination of kamma nor cessation of kamma will be kammanirodha, a plain self-contradiction, similar to the contradiction that saṅkhārā is asaṅkhata (nibbāna) . A “black is white” principle.

From this analysis, we see that the arahant’s actions cannot be designated as kamma without encountering a self-contradiction. One will need the ability to believe that “black is white” in order to justify the view that arahant’s actions by body, speech and mind can be designated as kamma.

Thus from these analyses, it is shown that:

  1. The arahant’s actions cannot be designated as kamma without contradicting the four noble truths.

  2. The arahant’s actions cannot be designated as kamma without proliferating that which ought not be proliferated, an unwarranted papañcā.

  3. The arahant’s actions cannot be designated as kamma without encountering a self-contradiction.

Why does any attempt to designate the actions of an arahant as kamma lead to self-contradictions, or contradictions with the Buddha’s teachings of the four noble truths, or unwarranted papañcā when subjected to scrutiny in light of the saddhamma? Because as the Buddha declares in the alagaddūpama Sutta (MN 22), “O bhikkhus, the Dhamma well proclaimed by me thus is clear, open, [self-] evident, and free of patchwork”. With the well proclaimed dhamma being clear, open, [self-] evident, and free of patchwork, any interpretations of the well proclaimed Dhamma based on arisen “other insignificant teachings” (uppannaṃ parappavādaṃ), like the abhidhamma doctrine, is easily exposed as patchwork when subjected to analysis. Such wrong interpretations are bound to lead to inconsistencies in the well proclaimed Dhamma which is clear, open, [self-] evident, and free of patchwork.

At the end of the exposition of the kammanirodha sutta, because of its profundity, the Buddha admonishes the bhikkhus to resort to root of trees and empty huts to cultivate jhāna, that they may attain arahantship through the full comprehension of old kamma, the abandoning of new kamma, the realization of liberation through the cessation of kamma, thus bringing the noble eightfold path to fulfillment by development.

But why does the Buddha ask the disciples to go through all the trouble of resorting to root of trees and empty huts to cultivate jhāna in order to understand kamma, something which is nowadays easily understandable according to the abhidhamma doctrine, with re-defined sutta terms like kiriya, kammavipāka, etc and new abhidhamma terms like paṭisandhi viññāṇa, khandhaparinibbāna, abyākata, etc? If the abhidhamma can attempt to simplify the Buddha’s atakkāvacara teachings to make the teachings understandable enough for commentators to write their commentaries about the arahants as if they are experts on the workings of kamma, then why can’t we also simplify the Buddha’s atakkāvacara teachings to our level of understanding so we can write our own commentaries about the arahants? After all, to our naked eyes (if we are fortunate enough to bless our eyes on the sight of an arahant), it looks like the arahant performs actions with body, speech or mind just like us, so why not designate those actions as kamma or kiriya, just like we designate our own actions? Why not simplify the dhamma to fit our understanding based on our own experiences, even if our experiences are not in the range of the arahant’s experience? Why keep the arahant a mystery if he eats and walks and talks and feels like us?

Perhaps Venerable Nandaka’s simile from the nandakovāda sutta (MN 146, Venerable Bodhi’s translation) can help us to appreciate how the looks of an arahant could be deceiving.

“Sisters, suppose a skilled butcher or his apprentice were to kill a cow and carve it up with a sharp butcher’s knife. Without damaging the inner mass of flesh and without damaging the outer hide, he would cut, sever, and carve away the inner tendons, sinews, and ligaments with the sharp butcher’s knife, and having cut, severed, and carved all this away, he would remove the outer hide and cover the cow again with that same hide. Would he be speaking rightly if he were to say: ‘This cow is joined to this hide just as it was before’?”

“No, venerable sir. Why is that? Because if that skilled butcher or his apprentice were to kill a cow and carve it up with a sharp butcher’s knife, without damaging the inner mass of flesh and without damaging the outer hide, and would cut, sever, and carve away the inner tendons, sinews, and ligaments with the sharp butcher’s knife, and having cut, severed, and carved all this away, even though he covers the cow again with that same hide and says: ‘This cow is joined to this hide just as it was before,’ that cow would still be disjoined from that hide.”

“Sisters, I have given this simile in order to convey a meaning. This is the meaning: ‘The inner mass of flesh’ is a term for the six internal bases. ‘The outer hide’ is a term for the six external bases. ‘The inner tendons, sinews, and ligaments’ is a term for delight and lust. ‘The sharp butcher’s knife’ is a term for noble wisdom—the noble wisdom that cuts, severs, and carves away the inner defilements, fetters, and bonds.

Also in the cetanā sutta (AN 4.171), the Buddha describes how action (by body, speech and mind) does not apply to the living arahant, since he has no more ignorance:

“When there is the body, O Bhikkhus, with bodily intention as cause, there arises inward pleasure and pain; when there is speech, O Bhikkhus, with verbal intention as cause, there arises inward pleasure and pain; when there is the mind, O Bhikkhus, with mental intention as cause, there arises inward pleasure and pain, all conditioned by ignorance.”

…

“In all these cases, O Bhikkhus, ignorance hangs on. But with the remainderless fading away and cessation of ignorance, that body does not exist conditioned by which there arises inward pleasure and pain; that speech does not exist conditioned by which there arises inward pleasure and pain; that mind does not exist conditioned by which there arises inward pleasure and pain. That field (khettaṃ) does not exist, that ground (vatthum) does not exist, that base (ayatanam) does not exist, that case-because-of-which (adhikaraṇaṃ) does not exist conditioned by which there arises inward pleasure and pain.

As the Buddha says in the aggivaccha sutta (MN 72):

evameva kho, vaccha, yena rūpena tathāgataṁ paññāpayamāno paññāpeyya taṁ rūpaṁ tathāgatassa pahīnaṁ ucchinnamūlaṁ tālāvatthukataṁ anabhāvaṁkataṁ āyatiṁ anuppādadhammaṁ. rūpasankhayavimutto kho, vaccha, tathāgato gambhīro appameyyo duppariyogāḷho — seyyathāpi mahāsamuddo.

So too, O Vaccha, that form by which one designating the Tathagata would designate him has been abandoned by the Tathagata. He has cut it off at the root, made like a palm stump, obliterated so that it is no longer subject to future arising. The Tathagata is liberated from reckoning in terms of form, O Vaccha, he is profound, immeasurable, hard to fathom like the great ocean.

In the same way, that body (form) by which one designating the bodily action of an arahant would designate it has been abandoned by the arahant. He has cut it off at the root, made it like a palm stump, obliterated it so that it is no longer subject to future arising. The arahant is liberated from reckoning in terms of body (form), he is profound, immeasurable, hard to fathom like the great ocean. Designating his bodily action as “kamma” does not apply. Designating his bodily action as “kiriya” also does not apply. The same applies to the arahant’s verbal actions and mental actions. For what “doings” could be designated to the one for whom “done is what is to be done” (kataṃ karaṇīyaṃ). What “doings” could be designated to the one for whom “there is nothing further to be done” (natthi kiñci uttari karaṇīyaṃ)? What “doings” could be designated to the one for whom “there is no addition of what is done” (natthi katassa paṭicayo)?
