In the article, Where the Path Divides: Early Buddhism and the Bodhisattva Ideal, the venerable Bhikkhu Bodhi asks the following questions:

In the Nikayas we often see monks approach the Buddha and ask for advice on how to attain arahantship. So it seems reasonable to suppose that those who aspired to Buddhahood would have done the same and that the Buddha would have responded in a fitting way. The question then arises why we don’t find even a trace of teachings on the bodhisattva path in the archaic texts. If there is a causal background to the realization of enlightenment as a buddha rather than as an arahant, why are the Nikayas silent about it? Why don’t they point to the possibility of buddhahood itself as an object of aspiration? Why do they include no description of a bodhisattva career?
–DN 28

Such a question from a Buddha’s disciple to the Buddha about a bodhisatta path is not found in the early Nikayas because the Buddha’s disciples understood quite well that the sambodhi of a stream-enterer in his last life is no different from the sambodhi of a bodhisatta in his last life. In the janavasabha (DN 18), the Buddha defines stream-enterers as:

tiṇṇaṃ saññojanānaṃ parikkhayā sotāpannā avinipātadhammā niyatā sambodhiparāyaṇāti.

From the complete extinction of the three fetters, they are stream-enterers, not subject to the woeful plane, on a fixed course, destined for supreme awakening (sambodhi).

In the same text, the venerable Ananda expresses where the Buddha gained his supreme awakening (sambodhi) as follows:

bhagavato kho pana sambodhi magadhesu. yattha kho bhagavato sambodhi magadhesu, kathaṃ tattha bhagavā māgadhake paricārake abbhatīte kālakate upapattīsu na byākareyya?

Besides, the Auspicious One became supremely awakened (sambodhi) in Magadha. [Since] the place where the Auspicious One became supremely awakened (sambodhi) was in Magadha, why wouldn’t the Auspicious One declare the birth of the Magadhans disciples who have died and passed away in that place?
–DN 18

Now let us suppose that there was a bhikkhu who was clueless about the path to sambodhi. If he were to approached the Buddha and ask about the path to sambodhi, with aspirations for Buddhahood, how would he word his question to the Buddha?

sambodhigāmī, sambodhigāmī’ti, bhante, vuccati. katamaṃ nu kho bhante sambodhigāmī?

Leading to supreme awakening, leading to supreme awakening, O Bhante, it is said. What, O Bhante, is leading to supreme awakening spoken of?

The potential answer is adapted from the kalyāṇasīla sutta (IT 97), where the Buddha defines kalyāṇadhammo as one who dwells devoted to the enculturation of the seven sets of bodhipakkhiyā dhammas, which are also called the seven leading to supreme awakening (satta sambodhigāmino).

satta sambodhigāmino’ti kho bhikkhu, sambodhigāmī’ti vuccati. katame satta? cattāro satipaṭṭhānā, cattāro sammappadhānā, cattāro iddhipādā, pañca indriyāni, pañca balāni, satta bojjhaṅgā, ariyo aṭṭhaṅgiko maggo. satta sambodhigāmino’ti kho bhikkhu, sambodhigāmī’ti vuccatī’ti.

The seven leading to supreme awakening, O bhikkhu, is what leading to supreme awakening is spoken of. What seven? The four establishments of mindfulness, the four right strivings, the four bases for psychic powers, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven awakening factors, the noble eight-fold path. The seven leading to supreme awakening, O bhikkhu, is what leading to supreme awakening is spoken of.

In the mahaparinibbana sutta (DN 16), the Buddha declares that the seven sets of bodhipakkhiyā dhammas are the dhammas he has taught his noble disciples to enculturate after having realized them with supreme knowledge. Only sekhas and arahants possess the abhinna needed to enculturate the seven sets of bodhipakkhiyā dhammas as declared in the mulapariyaya sutta (MN 1). If the seven sets of bodhipakkhiyā is the path to supreme awakening as declared by the Buddha, then who was the bodhisatta?” Bodhi means awakened and satta means a being. So a bodhisatta is an awakened being. An ariya, not a puthujjana. A “puthujjana bodhisatta” is simply a contradiction in terms. As noted above, a sotāpanna is defined as one who is “no longer subject to the lower destinations, on a fixed course, destined for supreme awakening (avinipātadhammo, niyato, sambhodi parāyaṇo); and a bodhisatta is defined as one who is just an awakened being, not yet fully awakened to supreme awakening (sambodhā anabhisambuddhassa bodhisattasseva). Thus, both a sekha and a bodhisatta have supreme awakening as their destination.

Paramis: The Bait of Mara

The major Buddhist traditions have developed their own bodhisatta path based on fulfilling paramis. In the Theravada tradition, one is required to fulfill ten paramis for different durations depending on the type of arahantship one aspires to whether sammasambuddha arahantship, paccekhabuddha arahantship, chief disciple arahantship or regular disciple arahantship. For one’s aspiration to come true, one has to make a vow in the presence of a living Buddha. Likewise, the Mahayana tradition proposes making a vow to fulfill six paramis for Buddhahood. It is not necessary to make such a vow in the presence of a living Buddha. However in the suttas, the Buddha advises against such undertakings for future becoming as a trap of Mara in the yavakalāpi sutta (SN 35.248).

Suppose O bhikkhus, that a sheaf of barley were to be laid down at the cross roads. Then six men would come with flail in hand. They would strike those sheaf of barley with the six flails. Thus indeed, O bhikkhus, that sheaf of barley would be well-beaten being struck with the six flails. Then a seventh man would come with flail in hand. He would strike that sheaf of barley with the seventh flail. Thus indeed O bhikkhus, that sheaf of barley would be even more thorougly well-beaten being struck with the seventh flail.

So too, O bhikkhus, the Dhamma-deaf puthujjana is struck in the eye with agreeable and disagreeable forms, struck in the ear with agreeable and disagree- able sounds, struck in the nose with agreeable and dis- agreeable aromas, struck in the tongue with agreeable and disagreeable flavors, struck in the body with agree- able and disagreeable tangibles, struck in the mind with agreeable and disagreeable dhammas. If, O bhikkhus, that Dhamma-deaf puthujjana is intent on a future re- newal of being, then indeed, O bhikkhus, that empty man is more thorougly well-beaten, just as, O bhikkhus, that sheaf of barley having been even more thorougly well-beaten, being struck with the seventh flail.

In the suttas, the Buddha interrogates proponents of doctrines that proclaim a path to liberation based on what was done in the past or what will be done in the future. For example in the devadaha sutta (MN 101), since the Niganthas teach the termination of kamma accumulated in past lives as a path to liberation from suffering, the fact that their practice does not allow any way of measuring their progress towards liberation from suffering means that they teach a path to that which they do not know, to that which they do not see.

But because, O friend Niganthas, you do not know: “Indeed, we were in the past. It is not the case that we were not.” You do not know: “Indeed we did evil kamma in the past. It is not the case that we did not.” You do not know: “We did such or such evil kamma in the past.” You do not know: “So much suffering has been worn away or so much suffering remains to be worn away, or certainly by the wearing away of so much suffering, all suffering will be worn away.” You do not know the abandoning of unwholesome dhammas and the taking up of wholesome dhammas in this very life. That being so, it would not be proper for the venerable Niganthas to declare: “Whatever this individual person experiences…
–MN 101

This criticm also applies to proponents of the path of paramis since they do not know whether they have made a vow in the past, how much paramis that have already accumulated in the past and how much paramis remain to be fulfilled. The Buddha compares this way of teaching to a file of blind men in the tevijja sutta (DN 13). The wish to put the ripening of accumulated good kamma on hold to continuously accumulate paramis is declared not the purpose of the life of purity under the Auspicious One as stated in the mahakotthita sutta (AN 9.13):

The Buddha’s doctrine on paths to liberation that rely on vows or aspirations is stated in the bhumija sutta (MN 126) as follows:

If, having made an aspiration, or having made no aspiration, or having made both an aspiration and no aspiration, or having made neither an aspiration nor non-aspiration, one leads the life of purity not [based on] the root cause [for purity], one is incapable of attaining any fruit. On the other hand, if, having made an aspiration, or having made no aspiration, or having made both an aspiration and no aspiration, or having made neither an aspiration nor non-aspiration, one leads the life of purity [based on] the root cause [for purity], one is capable of attaining fruits.

Is there any sutta bases that motivated this teaching about the bodhisatta’s path as involving fulfilling paramis? Perhaps the lakkhana sutta (DN 30), where the Buddha describes what past kamma led to the auspicious signs of a great man and the corresponding qualities of a Buddha, is what led to the idea of fulfilling paramis. The Buddha clarifies in the sallekha sutta (MN 8) with the famous simile of one sinking in the mud, he explains that it is not possible for one sinking in the mud to help another who is sinking in the mud. Likewise in his teachings, it is not possible that one who is himself untamed, undisciplined, not attained nibbāna, can tame another, discipline another, or help another to attain nibbāna; but it is possible that one who is himself tamed, disciplined, attained nibbāna, can tame another, discipline another, and help another to attain nibbāna.

In the bahujanahita sutta (IT 84), the Buddha singles out three types of persons who are capable of giving the Dhamma offering as those who arise in the world for the welfare of many, for the happiness of many, out of compassion for the world, for the good, welfare, and happiness of devas and humans. They are a Buddha, an arahant and a sekha. Only these three types of persons have arrived at the true Dhamma (saddhamma) and can distinguish the true Dhamma from the counterfeit dhamma (patirupaka dhamma). They are the only ones who are capable of explaining the well-proclaimed Dhamma that is auspicious in the beginning, auspicious in the middle, auspicious in the end, with the correct meaning and phrasing, proclaiming the life of purity that is utterly complete and pure. They have pulled themselves out of the mud and stand on firm ground unsullied by the mud. It is only they who understand the difficulties and subtleties involved in getting out of the mud and can therefore guide others out of the mud.

This indeed was said by the Auspicious One, said by the Arahant, as heard by me.

The Bodhisatta Gotama declared at the time of his birth that "This is my last birth", without having attained sambodhi. That statement only holds true for a sotapanna on his last birth or a sakadagami who have returned to this world and are therefore guaranteed that this is their last birth.

What happens when a sotapanna returns to this world during a time when a Buddha’s sasana has not disappeared is described by Sakka in the sakkapanha sutta (DN 21):

‘Remaining just here, [the state of] deva has come to be for me, and gained is renewed life for me. You should know thus, dear Sir.’

“Seeing this first benefit, venerable sir, I declare such an acquisition of feeling, an acquisition of happiness.

‘Having passed from the deva group, having abandoned the non-human life, unconfused, I will enter a womb where my mind delights.’

“Seeing this second benefit, venerable sir, I declare such an acquisition of feeling, an acquisition of happiness.

“Seeing this third benefit, venerable sir, I declare such an acquisition of feeling, an acquisition of happiness.

So Sakka forsees either attaining arahantship or anagami in his next human birth. What about once-returner and one-seed attainer who return to the human world when the instructions of a Buddha has disappeared? They are still born with unconfused wisdom that can discriminate what is the goal and what is not the goal but they have to rediscover the path, since all previous instructions about the path are lost. As the Buddha describes of the noble disciples who are accomplished in view in the niṭṭhangata sutta (AN 10.6):

“Whoever, O bhikkhus has reached certainty about me, all those are accomplished in view. Of those who are accomplished in view, five reach perfection in this world. Five reach perfection having left this world. Which five reach perfection in this world? The seven-lives-at-most [attainer], clan-to-clan [attainer], one seed [attainer], a sakadagami, one who attains arahantship in just this very life. These five reach perfection here in this world.

Which five reach perfection having left this world? An attainer of nibbana between [death and arising], an attainer of nibbana upon arrival, an attainer of nibbana without effort, an attainer of nibbana with effort, the one bound upstream, heading to the Akanittha realm. These are the five who reach perfection having left this world. Whoever, O bhikkhus has reached certainty about me, all those are accomplished in view. Of those who are accomplished in view, five reach perfection in this world. Five reach perfection having left this world.”

And so it was with our Bodhisatta Gotama who lived the life of purity under the arahant Kassapa, the supremely-awakened Buddha proper (see ghaṭikāra sutta, MN 81) prior to arising in the tusita heaven. Even as a householder, Bodhisatta Gotama acknowledged not being a puthujjana when he said in the sukhumāla sutta, AN 3.36):

I was delicate, O bhikkhus, supremely delicate, excessively delicate. Indeed at my father’s residence, these lotus ponds were built purposely for me. In one entirely white lotuses were planted, in one red lotuses, in one blue lotuses. I, O bhikkhus, did not wear sandalwood that were not from Kasi. My turban, O bhikkhus, were from Kasi, my jackets were from Kasi, my inner robes were from Kasi, my outer robes were from Kasi. A white parasol, O bhikkhus, was held over me day and night [with the thought]: Let neither cold, nor heat, nor grass, nor dust, nor dew touch him.

For me, O bhikkhus, there were three mansions: one for the cold season, one for the hot season, one for the rainy season. During the four months of the rainy season, O bhikkhus, I amused myself in the rainy season mansion with entirely female musicians without descending to the lower floors of the mansion. And just as, O bhikkhus, at other residences, servants, labourers and employees were offered meal of rice husk porridge accompanied with sour gruel, in exactly the same way, O bhikkhus, at my father’s residence, servants, labourers and employees were offered meal of fine rice accompanied with meat.

Of me being endowed with such great fortune, O bhikkhus, with such delicacy, it occurred to me: “For the Dhamma-deaf puthujjana, himself being liable to old age, not exempt, having seen others who are old, becomes troubled, humiliated, disgusted, having overlooked his own [liability]. But if I, being liable to old age, not exempt, having seen others who are old, were to become troubled, humiliated, disgusted, that would not be proper for me.” Of me, O bhikkhus, reflecting thus, I abandoned all the youth’s intoxication with youth.
–AN 3.36

And in the bhayabherava sutta (MN 4), the Buddha acknowledges being an ariya as follows:

It occured to me thus, O Brahmin: Whatever samanas or brahmanas with perplexity and doubt make use of dwelling places in remote forest thickets, then indeed with perplexity and doubt as cause, those venerable samanas and brahmanas invoke unskillful fear and dread. But I am not one who makes use of dwelling places in remote forest thickets with perplexity or doubt. I have crossed over doubt. I am indeed a certain one among those ariyas gone beyond doubt who make use of dwelling places in remote forest thickets. Seeing this crossing over doubt within myself, O Brahmin, I dwelt even more unafraid in the forest.
–MN 4

We also get a glimpse of the Bodhisatta Gotama’s progress towards full awakening as an ariya in the kālī sutta (AN 10.26):

Some samanas and brahmanas, O sister, actualized the earth kasina as the highest goal. The Auspicious One understood with supreme knowledge, the extent to which that earth kasina is supreme. Having understood that with supreme knowledge, the Auspicious one saw the beginning, saw the danger, saw the escape, saw the knowledge and vision of the path and the non-path. [With] his vision of the beginning as cause, vision of danger as cause, vision of escape as cause, knowledge and vision of the path and the non-path as cause, he has known the attainment of the goal, the peace of the heart.
–AN 10.26

Because as an ariya, the Bodhisatta Gotama possessed the knowledge and vision of the path and the non-path, he knew that even attainments of the domain of nothingness and the domain of neither-perception-nor-non-perception which surpass the kasina attainments, were also not the supreme goal as taught by his former teachers Ālāra Kalāma and Udāka Rāmaputta, and therefore was able to surmount these attainments and awaken to the supreme awakening.

Now the knowledge of the overcoming of doubt is the hallmark of an ariya who is at least a stream enterer (sotapanna). Along with this knowledge, the knowledge and vision of the path and the non-path becomes the starting point (ādiṃ) of purification towards supreme awakening. It is very important to understand that the Bodhisatta Gotama was an ariya and not a puthujjana, since only an ariya is capable of enculturating the four establishments of mindfulness to enable the mind to become well established in them.

If the ariya abiding in this way attains arahantship without the help of an existing Buddha’s teaching and establishes an order of the four assemblies of bhikkhus, bhikkhunis, male lay disciples and female lay disciples, then he is a samma sambuddha. If he attains arahantship without the help of an existing Buddha’s teaching but does not establish an order of the four assemblies, then he is a paccekkha buddha.

This is how all the Auspicious Ones of the past, having abandoned the five hindrances, mental defilements that are weakeners of wisdom, and with a mind well established in the four establishments of mindfulness, enculturated the seven awakening factors and awakened to the supreme awakening proper.

This path treaded by all the Auspicious Ones of the past is the path of purification. It is just the same path that all those who have made the breakthrough to the four noble truths and crossed over doubt tread. Without making the breakthrough to the four noble truths, such purification that leads to the attainment of arahantship, and consequently attainment of buddhahood, is simply not possible, as made clear in the āmagandha sutta (Snp 2.2):

Neither by no fish and meat, nor by fasting,
neither by nakedness, nor by shaven-head;
neither by matted locks, nor being caked in mud,
neither by rough antelope hide, nor by devotion to fire worship;
or even those in the world, who mortify themselves in many ways for the immortal,
neither by incantation and fire oblations, nor devotion to sacrifices and seasonal ascetic practices,
can purify mortals, who have not crossed over doubt.
–Snp 2.2
